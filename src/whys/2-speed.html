Speed, memory and limits

Why about \( 10^8 \) steps per second?

A computer chip runs at a few GHz: a few \( 10^9 \) clock ticks per second.

A simple step of your code (add, compare, read the next cell) takes one tick or a few. The judge is shared and often older. Your loop also has extra work around each step.

So \( 10^8 \) is a safe estimate, not an exact law.

Some steps cost much more. Tests on one laptop:

whattime
\( 10^8 \) multiply-and-add0.08 s
\( 10^8 \) remainders %0.21 s
\( 2 \cdot 10^7 \) random jumps in a 128 MB array2.4 s

Random jumps in big memory are slow, because the chip waits for the memory.

What this means for you: under \( 10^8 \) simple steps, relax. Near \( 10^9 \), look for a faster idea.

Why are limits like \( 2 \cdot 10^5 \) so common?

Take \( n = 2 \cdot 10^5 \) and count the steps of two ideas.

  • \( n \log_2 n \approx 3.5 \cdot 10^6 \) steps: far below \( 10^8 \). Fast.
  • \( n^2 = 4 \cdot 10^{10} \) steps: about 400 times too many. Too slow.

Problem setters pick the limit so that the fast idea passes and the slow one fails. \( 10^5 \) and \( 2 \cdot 10^5 \) do this very clearly.

Other limits send similar signals: \( n \le 5000 \) allows \( n^2 \), and \( n \le 20 \) allows \( 2^n \).

What this means for you: read the biggest \( n \) first. It tells you the speed your idea needs.

Why does halving give \( \log n \)?

Play "guess my number" from 1 to 1000. Each yes/no question can cut the options in half.

1000, 500, 250, 125, … After 10 questions, one number is left.

The number of halvings to get down to 1 is \( \log_2 n \).

nhalvings
\( 10^3 \)10
\( 10^6 \)20
\( 10^9 \)30
\( 10^{18} \)60

The number grows 1000 times, but the questions grow by only 10.

What this means for you: whenever you can throw away half each step, the cost is tiny: about 30 steps for \( 10^9 \).

Why can sorting not beat \( n \log n \) by comparing?

10 items can stand in \( 10! = 3\,628\,800 \) different orders.

Each comparison gives a yes or a no. At best, it cuts the possible orders in half. To get from 3 628 800 orders down to 1 takes at least 22 halvings.

For \( n \) items this becomes about \( n \log_2 n \) comparisons. sort already reaches this speed.

Counting can be faster, because it does not compare. It works only when the values are small.

What this means for you: do not hunt for a faster general sort. sort is as fast as comparing can be.

Why 256 MB, and how many ints is that?

256 MB is the most common memory limit on Codeforces. 256 is \( 2^8 \), a round number for a computer.

256 MB is \( 256 \cdot 2^{20} = 268\,435\,456 \) bytes.

An int takes 4 bytes. So 256 MB holds 67 108 864 ints, about \( 6.7 \cdot 10^7 \). A long long takes 8 bytes: half as many.

Your program needs some memory for itself too. Do not plan to use all of it.

What this means for you: an array of \( 10^7 \) ints (40 MB) is fine. \( 10^8 \) ints (400 MB) is too much.

Why does very deep recursion crash?

Each call of a function gets a small piece of memory on the "stack". It keeps the call's local variables.

These pieces pile up until the calls return. On many Linux computers the stack is only 8 MB.

A test with a tiny recursive function and an 8 MB stack: depth \( 10^5 \) worked, depth \( 10^6 \) crashed.

Codeforces gives programs a much bigger stack. So code can work there and crash on your computer.

What this means for you: a depth up to about \( 10^5 \) is usually fine. If your computer crashes on a deep test, the stack may be the reason, not your idea.

Why is endl slow?

cout does not send each character out at once. It collects text in a "buffer" and sends a big piece at a time.

The word endl writes a new line and then forces cout to send everything now. Sending is the slow part.

Test: printing \( 10^6 \) lines into a file on Linux. With endl: 0.82 s. With '\n': 0.03 s.

With \( 10^6 \) lines, this difference alone can cause "Time limit exceeded".

What this means for you: end lines with '\n'. Never use endl in a loop.

Why does ios::sync_with_stdio(false) make cin fast?

C++ has two sets of input tools: cin/cout, and the older scanf/printf from C.

By default, cin works in step with the C tools, so the order stays right if you mix them. That costs time.

ios::sync_with_stdio(false); turns this off. cin.tie(nullptr); stops cin from flushing cout before each read.

Reading \( 10^6 \) numbers on Linux took 0.10 s normally and 0.027 s with the two lines.

After these two lines, do not mix cin with scanf or cout with printf.

What this means for you: put the two lines at the top of main and use only cin/cout.

Why can unordered_map be attacked on Codeforces?

unordered_map puts each key into a "bucket" that a hash function picks.

Usually the keys spread over many buckets, and each operation is fast. But for whole numbers, the g++ hash is the number itself. The bucket is that number modulo the bucket count.

Someone who knows this can choose keys that all land in one bucket. Then every operation walks a long list.

With \( 2 \cdot 10^5 \) keys, normal keys took 0.006 s and colliding keys took 7.3 s.

On Codeforces, other people can send such a test against your solution.

What this means for you: use map by default. It is always \( O(\log n) \) per operation.

Why is it called "Big O"?

The O stands for "order": the order of growth of a function.

The sign is older than computers. Paul Bachmann used it in a book on number theory in 1894. Edmund Landau made it popular soon after.

Later, computer scientists borrowed it to describe how fast the number of steps grows.

\( O(n^2) \) means: the steps grow like \( n^2 \). We ignore constant factors.

What this means for you: read \( O(\dots) \) as "grows like". It is a size class, not an exact count.
