Remainders and primes

Why \( 10^9 + 7 \)?

Some answers are huge, like the number of ways to do something. So the problem asks only for the remainder after dividing by 1 000 000 007.

This number is chosen for four reasons. All four are checked with real code.

  • It is a prime. That makes division possible in a special way you learn later.
  • It is below \( 2^{30} = 1\,073\,741\,824 \), so every remainder fits in an int.
  • The sum of two remainders is at most 2 000 000 012. That is below the int limit 2 147 483 647.
  • The product of two remainders is below \( 1.0000001 \cdot 10^{18} \). That fits in long long.

Careful: the sum of three remainders can pass the int limit.

What this means for you: take % MOD after every addition and every multiplication. Multiply in long long.

Why does 998 244 353 appear too?

998 244 353 is also a prime below \( 2^{30} \). So everything in the \( 10^9 + 7 \) story is true for it too.

It has a special form: \( 119 \cdot 2^{23} + 1 \). This form helps a fast trick for multiplying polynomials.

You do not need that trick now.

What this means for you: treat 998 244 353 exactly like \( 10^9 + 7 \).

Why stop at \( \sqrt{n} \) when you look for divisors?

Divisors come in pairs. For 36: \( 1 \cdot 36 \), \( 2 \cdot 18 \), \( 3 \cdot 12 \), \( 4 \cdot 9 \), \( 6 \cdot 6 \).

In every pair, one number is at most 6, and \( \sqrt{36} = 6 \). So if you find no divisor up to \( \sqrt{n} \), there is none above it either.

For \( n = 10^9 \), that is about 31 600 checks, not \( 10^9 \).

Write the test as d * d <= n, not with sqrt. So you stay with whole numbers.

What this means for you: checking one number up to \( 10^9 \) or \( 10^{12} \) is fast. Use long long d when \( n \) is big.

How old is the sieve of Eratosthenes?

It is named after Eratosthenes, a Greek scholar who lived about 2200 years ago, in the 3rd century BC.

He worked in Alexandria, in Egypt. He is also famous for measuring the size of the Earth.

We know the method from a later book by Nicomachus, written about 300 years after him.

The idea: cross out the multiples of each prime. What stays is prime.

What this means for you: an idea this old is still the simple, fast way to list all primes up to about \( 10^7 \).

Why is Euclid's algorithm still used after 2300 years?

Euclid's book "Elements" was written around 300 BC. It describes a way to find the greatest common divisor (gcd) of two numbers.

Today we write it like this: \( \gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b) \), until the second number is 0.

For 48 and 18: \( 48 \bmod 18 = 12 \), \( 18 \bmod 12 = 6 \), \( 12 \bmod 6 = 0 \). The gcd is 6.

Many people call it the oldest real algorithm still in use today.

It is very fast: the number of steps grows like a log, not like the numbers.

What this means for you: C++ gcd(a, b) is this method. Call it without fear, even inside a loop.
