Names and history

Why "bug" and "debugging"?

Engineers called a hidden fault a "bug" long before computers. Thomas Edison used the word in his letters in the 1870s.

In 1947, the team of the Harvard Mark II computer found a moth stuck inside one of its parts. They taped it into their logbook with the note "First actual case of bug being found".

The joke was that the word was already common, and this time it was a real insect. The page is now in a museum in Washington.

"Debugging" means taking the bugs out.

What this means for you: bugs are normal for everyone. Finding them fast is a skill you can learn.

Why is it called "greedy"?

A greedy person takes the biggest piece now and does not think about later.

A greedy algorithm does the same: at each step it takes the choice that looks best now, and never goes back.

Sometimes this is right. Sometimes it is not. Coins 1, 3 and 4, and you must pay 6: greedy takes \( 4 + 1 + 1 \), three coins. The best is \( 3 + 3 \), two coins.

Jack Edmonds used the name in the title of a well-known paper in 1971.

Do not trust a greedy idea before you test it on small examples.

What this means for you: greedy is fast and short. Check it against brute force on small cases before you submit.

Why is it called "dynamic programming"?

Richard Bellman developed the method in the 1950s, at the RAND Corporation in the USA.

At that time, "programming" meant planning and making schedules, not writing code.

In his autobiography (1984), Bellman says he picked "dynamic" because it sounded good and hid the mathematics. An important man in the government, he wrote, disliked the word "research".

This is his own account. Some historians question details of the dates.

What matters: the name tells you nothing about the method.

What this means for you: think of DP as "fill a table of answers to smaller questions". Ignore the name.

Who was Dijkstra?

Edsger W. Dijkstra (1930–2002) was a Dutch computer scientist. The name sounds like "DIKE-stra".

He designed his shortest-path algorithm in 1956 and published it in 1959.

In an interview many years later, he told how it happened. He designed it in about twenty minutes at a café in Amsterdam, without pen and paper.

He wanted a problem that people without computer knowledge could understand: the shortest road between two Dutch cities.

He received the Turing Award in 1972, the top prize in computer science.

What this means for you: a great idea can be small. Dijkstra's algorithm is a short loop with a priority queue.

Why "stack" and "queue"?

A stack is like a pile of plates: you put a plate on top, and you take from the top. The last one in is the first one out.

A queue is like a line of people at a shop: first come, first served.

What this means for you: ask "who must come out next: the newest or the oldest?" Newest: stack. Oldest: queue.

Where do graphs come from?

In 1736, Leonhard Euler looked at the city of Königsberg. A river split it into four land areas, joined by seven bridges.

The question: can you walk through the city and cross every bridge exactly once?

Euler drew each land area as a point and each bridge as a line. Only "what is connected to what" was left.

Then he showed it is impossible, because each of the four areas has an odd number of bridges. This is often called the start of graph theory.

What this means for you: to use a graph, keep only the points and the links. Throw away the rest of the story.

Which binary search bug hid for years?

Many binary searches compute the middle as (lo + hi) / 2.

If lo and hi are big, lo + hi passes the int limit. For lo = 2 000 000 000 and hi = 2 100 000 000, the sum is \( 4.1 \cdot 10^9 \).

In 2006, Joshua Bloch of Google wrote that Java's own binary search had this bug for about nine years. Many books had the same line.

Nobody noticed, because it shows only with huge arrays or huge values.

The safe line lo + (hi - lo) / 2 gives 2 050 000 000, the right middle.

What this means for you: write lo + (hi - lo) / 2, and use long long when the range is big.

What is bits/stdc++.h?

It is a file that comes with the g++ compiler. It includes the whole C++ standard library at once.

It is not part of the C++ standard. Some compilers, like Microsoft's MSVC, do not have it.

Compiling takes a bit longer, because the compiler reads the whole library. In a contest, nobody cares.

The G++ compilers on Codeforces have it.

What this means for you: in contests, choose a G++ compiler and use this one line. In big projects, people include only what they need.
