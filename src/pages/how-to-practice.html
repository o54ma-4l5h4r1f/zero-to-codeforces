Solve a few problems a little above your level, every day. Think first, read the solution late, then write the code yourself.

Before this lesson

  • You know contests, divisions and rating → How Codeforces works
  • You can test your code on the samples → Your C++ template

The practice loop

  1. Pick a problem at your rating, or up to 200 above it. No rating yet? Start with 800.
  2. Think for 20–40 minutes. Use paper. Try small examples by hand.
  3. Stuck? Open the editorial. Read only until you get the idea. Then close it.
  4. Write the code yourself and get Accepted. Reading a solution is not solving. The code you write yourself is what teaches you.
  5. Write one line in your notebook: the trick you learned.

How to pick problems

Open the Problemset. Set the rating filter, for example 800 to 1000. Then sort by the number of people who solved each problem.

The practice ladders in this guide are sorted by the official rating, then by how many people solved each problem. So the top of a ladder is the easiest and the most-solved. More solvers also mean a fairer rating.

Finished a ladder? The site has a Training page. It has more real problems for each lesson, from easy to hard. Its Mixed set hides the topic, like a real contest.

What strong coders say

This advice comes from the most-liked practice posts on Codeforces. Many authors agree on these points.

  • Practice a little above your level. Easy problems feel good but teach little. Aim to solve about one in three to one in two alone.
  • Be honest with yourself. The biggest trap is to "think" for some minutes without really trying, and then read the solution. Real effort matters more than hours or problem counts.
  • When to read the editorial? People do not agree. Some say: never, come back later. Others say: after 10–30 minutes with no new idea. All agree on one thing: do not open it while you still have ideas to try.
  • After the editorial, write the code yourself. Never copy it.
  • Upsolve after every contest. Upsolving means: after the contest, solve the first problem you could not solve. It sits exactly at the edge of your level.
  • Virtual contests count, if you take them seriously and upsolve after. Contests train speed. The archive trains solving.
  • Learn a new algorithm when problems ask for it. Most people are stuck because of thinking, not because a topic is missing.
  • Learn to stress test. One post says it is worth more than 100 rating points. You learn it in the next lesson.
  • First by topic, then mixed. Beginners learn one topic at a time. Then hide the tags: in a contest nobody tells you the topic.

What you can skip on the way to 1600

Do not stall on a hard lesson. Three lessons of Stage 3 are optional below 1600:

  • DSU (union-find),
  • Topological sort,
  • Interactive problems.

No practice post we read lists them as needed below 1600. Read them later, or when a problem asks for one. Big tools like segment trees are not in this guide at all. They come after 1600.

Contests

  • Join every Div. 4, Div. 3 and Educational round. They are made for learning. Join Div. 2 rounds too when you have time.
  • No contest at a good time for you? Use virtual participation: an old contest with a real timer. It does not change your rating.
  • In a contest, solve in order: A, then B, then C. Do not stay on one problem for the whole time.
  • Your rating goes up and down. This is normal. Ignore it for your first 20 contests. Count solved problems instead.

About 1–2 hours a day. A little every day beats a lot on one day. Move the days to fit your week.

Solve one together

Problem. Last week you tried 12 problems, 4 at each rating. At 800 you solved 4 of 4, each in about 5 minutes. At 900 you solved 2 of 4 alone. At 1000 you solved 0 of 4, and even the editorials were not clear. Where do you practice this week?

  1. What is asked? Find the rating where you solve about one in three to one in two alone.
  2. Look at each rating.
    RatingSolved aloneShareMeaning
    8004 of 4alltoo easy: you learn little
    9002 of 4one in twothe best level
    10000 of 4nonetoo far for now
  3. The plan. Practice at 900 this week, plus one 1000 problem each day. Upsolve the 1000 problems after you read their editorials.
  4. Check again next week. When 900 feels like 800 did, move up by 100.

Wrong ideas people get

  • You may think: The more problems I solve, the better I get, even if they are easy.

    The truth: Easy problems teach you nothing new. Problems a little too hard for you move you forward.

  • You may think: To reach 1600 I must learn every advanced algorithm.

    The truth: Up to 1600 you need the basics, done well. Thinking matters more than the number of topics.

  • You may think: My rating went down after one contest, so I got worse.

    The truth: One contest has a lot of luck in it. Look at your solved problems over a month.

Common mistakes

  • You open the editorial after 5 minutes. The thinking time is the training.
  • You read the editorial and say "I understand", but you do not write the code.
  • You watch lessons for weeks and solve nothing. One lesson, then problems.

Not now

  • The three optional lessons. Come back when you are near 1600, or when a problem needs one: DSU (union-find), Topological sort, Interactive problems.
  • Segment trees and other big tools. Many strong coders say they learned them too early. Wait until after 1600: After 1600.

Check yourself

  1. You solve every 1100 problem alone in 10 minutes. What do you do?
    Answer

    Go up by 100, to 1200. At 1100 you learn little now.

  2. After a contest you could not solve problem C. What do you do the next day?
    Answer

    Upsolve C: think again, read the editorial if needed, and write the code yourself.

  3. Your rating is 1350 and the DSU lesson is hard for you. Must you stop until you get it?
    Answer

    No. DSU is optional below 1600. Go on with the other lessons and come back later.
