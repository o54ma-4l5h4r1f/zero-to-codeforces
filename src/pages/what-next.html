The map

Three good places to learn. The tables say where to look for each topic.

  • CPA = cp-algorithms.com — short text and code for almost every algorithm.
  • USACO = USACO Guide — lessons in order (Silver, Gold, Platinum) with chosen problems.
  • EDU = Codeforces EDU — free video lessons with practice problems.

The column "you need this when…" gives a clear signal in a statement. Do not learn a topic before you meet its signal a few times.

Small topics the lessons sent here

  • Sorting algorithms by hand (bubble, insertion, selection, quick, heap and merge sort). Writing them yourself is for university exams and job interviews. You do not need it for Codeforces. Use sort. If you are curious, read CPA "Sorting".
  • Binary search on real numbers and ternary search: CPA "Binary search" and "Ternary search".
  • DSU with removed edges (process the operations from the end, or DSU with rollback): CPA "Disjoint Set Union".
  • Topological sort with DFS: CPA "Topological Sorting". Kahn's queue from the lesson is enough.
  • Proofs of Fermat's rule and of "stars and bars", and inclusion–exclusion: CPA "Algebra" and "Combinatorics".
  • Harder interactive problems: solve the interactive problems of Div. 2 C and D after each contest.
  • Matrix power, the smallest-prime-factor sieve, fast prime tests, meet in the middle (\( n \) up to 40): CPA, by name.
  • Making a DP faster (less memory, fewer cells): USACO Gold, and the AtCoder DP contest after task F.
  • The maximum of every window with a deque: CPA "Minimum stack / Minimum queue".
  • How negative numbers are stored (why ~12 is -13), unsigned and __int128: CPA "Bit manipulation". long long is enough below 1600.
  • The exact math definition of \( O \) and full proofs of constructive patterns: the CSES book, chapter 2, and the editorials.
  • Long templates with many shortcuts: look at strong coders' code after 1600. Keep your short template until then.

How to keep growing

  • From now on, contests and upsolving matter more than new topics. Most 1600–1900 problems need a good idea, not a new algorithm.
  • After every contest, solve the first problem you could not solve. This one habit gives the most rating.
  • Practice problems a little above your rating, not easy ones. Think hard before you open the editorial. After you read it, write the code yourself.
  • When a topic comes back again and again in editorials, that is the time to learn it. Then solve 5–10 problems on it.
  • Fast and correct on easy problems is half of your rating. Keep doing virtual contests.

Check yourself

  1. A problem: \( n \le 18 \) cities, visit all of them with the smallest cost. Which topic of the map fits?
    Answer

    Bitmask DP. \( n \) is tiny, and the state is the set of cities already visited.

  2. A problem mixes \( 2 \cdot 10^5 \) operations "change a[i]" and "sum of a[l..r]". Why are prefix sums not enough?
    Answer

    Each change would rebuild the prefix array: \( n \) steps each time. That is about \( 4 \cdot 10^{10} \) steps. Use a Fenwick tree or a segment tree.

  3. Must you learn to write quick sort yourself to reach 1600?
    Answer

    No. On Codeforces you call sort. Hand-written sorts are for exams and interviews.
